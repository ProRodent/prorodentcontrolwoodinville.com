What the inspection covers.

An inspection is the part of the job that decides everything else. A technician walks the exterior first, looking at the roofline, siding, vents, utility entries, foundation edge and anything stored against the house. Then the interior spaces come next, which means the attic, crawl space or daylight basement, garage, kitchen and any room where you noticed activity.

The aim is to answer four plain questions. Which animal is involved, how many signs of activity are fresh, where does it get in, and what is the simplest plan that will hold? You hear the answers on site, and you get the plan and price before any work starts.

What we look for on Woodinville properties.

Houses here share some patterns. Wet winters test every seam, so failed flashing, torn crawl space vent screens and soft spots in soffit boards turn up often. Many homes have attached garages with storage along the walls, which hides activity for months. Properties on hillsides and wooded lots have branches that touch the roof.

Landmarks and busy commercial areas matter for business inspections. A tasting room, restaurant or garden shop near wine country has different issues from a home, including delivery doors, stored goods, floor drains and shared walls. See commercial rodent control for what business accounts need.

  • Roofline, soffits, fascia and vent covers.
  • Siding-to-foundation joints and crawl space vents.
  • Utility penetrations for pipes, wires, cable and gas.
  • Garage door seals and the base of side doors.
  • Attic insulation, rafters and the area around the water heater.

What an honest estimate includes.

Whoever you hire, ask for the same things. The animal identified, the entry points listed, the number of visits expected, the materials to be used and a separate price for any cleanup or repair. If a quote arrives as one number with no explanation, ask what is in it.

Be wary of any provider who promises a result before looking, or who quotes a fixed price by phone for a problem that has not been seen. Rodent jobs vary too much for that. We say what we know, what we do not yet know and what the inspection will settle.

Inspections that are not complaints.

Not every inspection follows a noise in the attic. Sellers sometimes want to know the condition of a house before listing it, buyers want a second opinion on a flagged item, and property managers want a record for a building. We handle those the same way, with a walk, a clear summary and a plan.

How to prepare.

Before the technician arrives.

  • Clear a path to the attic hatch, crawl space door and garage walls.
  • Note where you saw or heard activity and at what time.
  • Gather photos of droppings, gnawing or damage.
  • Keep pets away from the areas to be inspected.

Most inspections take under an hour for a typical house, with larger properties taking longer. Afterward you will know whether the answer is a quick trapping round, a sealing job, a cleanup or a mix of all three.

Tell us on the call

  • Where you noticed signs and how long ago.
  • The age of the house and any recent roof or siding work.
  • Whether there is a crawl space, daylight basement or attic access.
  • Any need for written documentation.

Before we arrive

  • Keep everyone away from visible nesting material and do not shake out stored boxes.
  • Skip bleach and sprays on dry droppings until the plan is set.
  • Resist sealing a gap you found until we have seen it, we want to know where animals travel.
  • Have a rough idea of how long the activity has been going on.
