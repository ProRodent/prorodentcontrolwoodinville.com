Why garages and basements get hit first.

A garage is the easiest door into a house. It is cluttered, rarely inspected and often kept cool and dry, which is exactly what a mouse looking for a winter nest wants. Daylight basements and lower-level storage rooms are similar. Boxes of holiday decorations, spare bedding, pet food and tools make perfect nest material and cover.

In Woodinville, many homes have attached garages, and some have daylight basements cut into slopes, so the lower level often touches soil, rock or a retaining wall. That contact gives rodents ground-level routes into the framing, especially on lots that border greenbelts or creek corridors.

What we check in the lower levels.

The inspection starts with the garage door. Seals that have flattened leave a gap along the slab, and the corners where the door meets the wall are the weakest spots. Then we look at side doors, the wall shared with the house, the sill plate, and any pipes, wires or ducts that pass through to the living space.

  • Garage door bottom seals and corner gaps.
  • Gaps around the door between garage and house.
  • Water heater and furnace closets, where warmth draws nesting.
  • Gaps around pipes, wires and gas lines.
  • Stored boxes, bags of seed or pet food, and wall-hugging shelves.
Stored boxes are a nest kit, and the garage is the showroom.

Freezers, boxes and stored food.

A chest freezer in the garage may look harmless, but the compressor area stays warm and the gap beneath it makes a good nest. Bags of birdseed, dog food and bulk dry goods in a cardboard box are easy to chew into. Moving them into lidded plastic or metal bins takes away both food and nesting material.

Fabric, paper and cardboard are the favorite nest materials. A stack of old boxes against a wall can hold a whole nest without you ever noticing, until the smell of urine or the sound of scratching gives it away.

How we deal with a lower-level problem.

Trapping goes along walls and behind stored items, with enclosed stations where pets or children are nearby. Once catches stop, we seal the garage door, the wall penetrations and any cracks at the slab edge. Our rodent exclusion page lists materials.

For vehicles parked in the garage, a nest in the engine bay is a separate matter, covered on our vehicle and car rodent removal page. If droppings have spread across a large area, see attic and crawl space cleanup for how cleanup works.

What you can do today.

Steps before the visit.

  • Move food, seed and pet food into sealed metal or hard plastic bins.
  • Pull boxes a few inches off the wall so we can see behind them.
  • Keep the garage door closed after dusk.
  • Do not sweep or vacuum droppings, and leave them for the technician.

Woodinville is known for wine, and home wine storage sometimes ends up in garages and basements, with cardboard cases stacked against the wall. Cardboard is nesting material. Keeping cases on a shelf or in a plastic tote reduces the risk.

Before we arrive

  • Clear a path to the attic hatch, the garage wall and the cabinet under the kitchen sink.
  • Leave droppings undisturbed, protective cleanup is part of the visit.
  • Avoid setting snap traps where children or pets can reach them.
  • Make a note of any odor, since smell can point to where an animal has died.

Questions about garage and basement rodent control

Why do mice love my garage?

It is dry, cluttered and rarely checked. Gaps at the door seal and stored boxes give them an easy way in and a ready-made nest.

Do I need to empty the garage?

No. Pulling boxes a few inches from the wall and moving food into sealed bins is usually enough for the inspection.

Can rodents reach the house from the garage?

Yes. Gaps around the door to the house, pipes and wires let them travel into walls and attics.

What about my daylight basement?

Lower levels that touch soil or retaining walls get similar treatment, and we check the same entry points.
