What callers describe.

Here are the usual first signs. None of them proves a problem alone, but two together almost always do.

  • A rat seen on a fence, wire or roofline at dusk.
  • Fruit or nuts gnawed in the yard.
  • Droppings in a shed or crawl space.
  • Holes about two inches wide beside a wall.
  • Scratching under floors or in the crawl space.

Edge-of-town properties.

Homes at the edge of a small town tend to have more yard and more cover than homes in a dense core. A lot with a shed, a woodpile or a vegetable garden gives rodents several places to settle before they reach the house. We walk the outdoor spaces first and tell you which ones matter.

Closer to the center, older homes and small commercial buildings share walls and alleys, and the plan leans toward sealing and waste handling instead of burrow work.

The local rodent picture.

Duvall is a small town set in the Snoqualmie River valley, where open land and river corridors are never far from the houses. Properties at the edge of town often back onto fields, wooded strips or drainage, and that edge setting is where rats and field mice start before they work toward a building.

Shared walls change the plan. In townhomes, condos and mixed-use buildings, rodents travel through voids between units, so one unit cannot solve it alone. Coordination with neighbors and the building manager makes the result last.

Trees and landscaping do a lot of the work for rodents. Branches that touch the roof become bridges, and dense plantings against the foundation give cover right at the wall. A short walk around the outside usually shows which plants and limbs matter.
