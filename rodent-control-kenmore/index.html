What residents report.

Callers describe their problems in different words, but the causes repeat. The list below reflects what we hear most often, and the inspection turns it into a plan.

  • Noises inside a wall at night.
  • Droppings along baseboards or on attic insulation.
  • Chewed pet food or birdseed.
  • Burrows beside a foundation, shed or deck.
  • A smell that gets stronger on warm days.

Water, trees and mid-century homes.

Older homes near the water often have crawl spaces with failing vent screens, and mature trees press against rooflines. Together they give rodents both a ground route and a canopy route.

We check both on every visit. If the house has a daylight basement, we look at where the foundation meets the soil.

Because many homes here were built before the city existed, ask a neighbor whether they have seen anything too. Rodents rarely stay on one lot.

Housing and setting.

Kenmore's homes sit between the lake, the river mouth and wooded hillsides. Many were built long before the city existed, so housing ages vary from mid-century to recent. Waterside properties and wooded streets have the heaviest vegetation, and those are where we usually find the most outdoor activity.

Garages and crawl spaces are the quiet back doors of most homes. Door seals flatten, vent screens tear, and stored boxes provide nest material. Those spaces are often unheated and rarely checked, so a problem can run for weeks before anyone notices.

Trees and landscaping do a lot of the work for rodents. Branches that touch the roof become bridges, and dense plantings against the foundation give cover right at the wall. A short walk around the outside usually shows which plants and limbs matter.

When rodent calls peak.

The first cold snap is a trigger. A sudden drop in temperature sends mice indoors within days, and garages and crawl spaces are the first places affected. If a cold night is forecast and you have not checked your seals, now is a good time.
