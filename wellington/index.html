What makes Wellington its own case

  • Homes of many ages

    West Wellington is a set of established residential streets, and each house has its own age and weak points.

  • Trees at the roofline

    Any tree that leans over a roofline or drops fruit and nuts can feed and bridge the way in for roof rats and squirrels.

  • Slopes and drainage

    East Wellington rises east of the valley, and water running downhill creates damp corridors along foundations.

Two halves with different characters.

Local real estate sources split Wellington into West and East. West Wellington is described as established residential streets west and northwest of downtown. East Wellington rises east of the valley and includes residential neighborhoods with varied lot sizes, so slope and drainage are worth checking there.

We use that split as a rough guide rather than a rule. The best predictor of a rodent problem is not the name of the neighborhood but the specific lot, its trees, its slope and the age and condition of the house. Our coverage data lists ZIP 98077 for Woodinville. Some Woodinville addresses are mailed under 98072, so if yours is, call and we will confirm coverage.

Older homes, older gaps.

Homes from different decades have different weak points. Older houses tend to have wood siding that has shifted against the foundation, crawl space vents with failing screens, and utility penetrations that were never sealed after upgrades. Homes from later decades may have attached garages, gaps around plumbing stack penetrations and roof-to-wall joints that open over time.

Remodels add a layer. New additions, decks and window changes sometimes leave hidden voids where old and new framing meet, which rodents can use for years. If your house has been added onto, mention it on the call.

Tree branches and the roofline.

A mature tree beside a house is a pleasure and also a bridge. Roof rats and squirrels use limbs to reach the roof, then follow the fascia and soffit to any gap. Walnut and fruit trees add food. We tell you what we see in the canopy and whether trimming back a few feet would help, and you decide how to proceed.

Often the shortest route to the attic is a branch.

How a Wellington visit runs.

We walk the exterior first, from the roofline down, then look at the crawl space, garage and attic. Because some lots slope, we also check how water leaves the house, since saturated soil along the foundation pushes rodents toward the walls.

If we find a nest, we plan trapping and removal before any sealing. After catches stop, exclusion work closes the gaps. We explain the plan and price before starting.

Timing the work across the year.

Autumn is the busiest season, as cooler, wetter weather pushes mice and rats toward buildings. Trim branches and check vents in late summer, before that push. In spring, listen for scratching, since nests started over winter get noisy when young animals grow.

Summer is the time to pick up fallen fruit and nuts and to clear brush from beside the house. A short walk around the outside every season catches most small gaps while they are still easy to fix.

A short list for Wellington homeowners.

Quick checks this week.

  • Look for branches within a few feet of the roof.
  • Check crawl space vent screens for tears or gaps.
  • Move firewood and compost away from the house.
  • Listen in the attic and garage after dusk.

If you find signs of a problem, call. Small fixes done early cost much less than repairs after a long infestation.

Before we arrive

  • Keep dogs and cats indoors or leashed if the activity is in the yard or garage.
  • Do not pour cleaners over droppings before the inspection.
  • Do not plug a hole yourself first, sealing it with an animal inside creates new problems.
  • Remember where you saw the animal and which direction it ran.
