Forest edges and hillside subdivisions.

Where subdivisions meet forest, animals move easily from the trees to the roofline. Branches, fences and woodpiles are the usual bridges. A house backed by a greenbelt is likely to see more mouse and squirrel activity than one on an open street.

A visit there usually starts at the lot edge and works inward.

If your home backs onto a creek or a trail, say so on the call, since those corridors shape where we place traps first.

The local rodent picture.

Issaquah lies at the south end of Lake Sammamish at the foot of forested hills. Its neighborhoods run from an older town center to hillside subdivisions backed by forest. Properties that border the hills and creeks get more outdoor rodent traffic, and the forest edge brings deer mice and squirrels along with rats.

Yards matter as much as houses. Bird feeders, compost, chicken feed, fruit that drops and woodpiles stacked against a wall all feed and shelter rodents. Changing a few of those habits often cuts activity before any trap is set.

Garages and crawl spaces are the quiet back doors of most homes. Door seals flatten, vent screens tear, and stored boxes provide nest material. Those spaces are often unheated and rarely checked, so a problem can run for weeks before anyone notices.

How the year shapes the problem.

Holiday storage brings its own risk. Cardboard boxes of decorations carried into a garage or attic in winter, then left for months, make ideal nests. Moving them into lidded totes and keeping them off the floor cuts the risk.

What the technician checks.

Next we look at the crawl space or lower level if the house has one. Torn vent screens, soiled insulation and burrows along the foundation tell us where activity is and which route the animals use to reach the living space.

The walk starts at the roofline, where soffits, vents and flashing show the most common entry points for roof rats and squirrels. From there we work down the walls to the foundation, noting every gap around pipes, wires and vents.
