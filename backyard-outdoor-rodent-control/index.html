Start with the yard, not the attic.

A yard is where most rodent problems begin. Rats and mice are drawn outside first by food, water and cover, and only move indoors when the weather turns or the yard becomes crowded. Fixing the yard cuts off the supply and removes the animals before they find a way into the house.

Many lots in and around Woodinville have gardens, trees and wooded edges, and some back onto greenbelts or creeks. That is lovely to live next to, and it also means rodents have a lot of cover close by.

What draws rodents to a yard.

  • Bird feeders, and the seed that drops beneath them.
  • Compost bins and piles that are open or poorly turned.
  • Chicken coops, feed bins and egg-laying areas.
  • Fruit and nuts left to fall and rot.
  • Woodpiles, tarps and clutter stacked against fences or the house.
  • Dense groundcover and ivy against the foundation.

Any one of these is manageable. A combination of several in one yard creates a reliable buffet. We walk the yard with you and point out which items matter most, so you can adjust the ones that are easy to change.

A yard with food, water and cover will always have visitors.

Burrows and runs.

Norway rats dig. Burrows show as holes about two inches across along foundations, under decks, beneath sheds and at the edges of garden beds. Fresh burrows have clean, smooth soil and no cobwebs. Along fences and walls, a worn, greasy path in the dirt marks a run.

We map active burrows and runs, then place traps along them, often inside covered stations to keep pets and wildlife out. Burrows are not filled until activity stops, so no animal is trapped underground. Afterward, we backfill and close the area as part of the plan.

Sheds, coops and outbuildings.

A shed full of stored items is a miniature house to a rodent. Gaps beneath the floor, at the base of the door and around vents invite them in. Chicken coops add feed and eggs, which are strong attractants. We inspect outbuildings with the same care as the house and recommend raised floors, metal feed bins and sturdy hardware cloth.

Some larger local properties include barns, sheds or other outbuildings. These structures need the same care as any shed, and they sit close to open ground.

Connecting yard work to the house.

Outdoor control works best alongside exclusion. We close the entry points on the house, covered on rodent exclusion, and treat the yard sources. For inside work, see rodent trapping and removal.

Simple habits help. Bring bird feeders in at night or switch to ones that catch seed. Keep lids on compost and bins. Store firewood off the ground and a few feet from the house. Trim shrubs back so that a gap shows daylight at the foundation.

Before we arrive

  • Keep dogs and cats indoors or leashed if the activity is in the yard or garage.
  • Do not pour cleaners over droppings before the inspection.
  • Do not plug a hole yourself first, sealing it with an animal inside creates new problems.
  • Remember where you saw the animal and which direction it ran.

Questions about backyard and outdoor rodent control

Is it safe to have traps in the yard with pets?

We use covered stations and place them where pets cannot reach. Tell us about dogs, cats and chickens on the call.

Do bird feeders really attract rats?

Spilled seed is a reliable food source. Bringing feeders in at night or using seed catchers helps a lot.

Will you fill the burrows?

Only after activity stops, so no animal is trapped inside. Then we close them up as part of the plan.

Can you handle chicken coops?

Yes. We inspect coops and recommend steps such as metal feed bins and sturdy mesh.
