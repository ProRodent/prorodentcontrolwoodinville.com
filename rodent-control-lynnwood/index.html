What we look for on local properties.

Lynnwood is a mostly suburban city built up around major roads, with shopping areas, apartment complexes, townhomes and single-family neighborhoods. Dense retail and multi-unit housing mean waste enclosures, shared walls and delivery doors, while quieter streets have yards and attached garages.

Housing age shapes where rodents get in. Older homes tend to have settled foundations, worn vents and utility penetrations that were never sealed. Newer homes have tighter siding, but gaps still appear at pipes, wires and the joints where roof meets wall. We check the building before we guess.

Commercial streets add food and waste. Restaurants, shops and delivery doors create steady supplies of cardboard and scraps, and dumpster pads with gaps at the base are common hot spots. Businesses are best served by monitoring and a written record.

Reading the property.

Next we look at the crawl space or lower level if the house has one. Torn vent screens, soiled insulation and burrows along the foundation tell us where activity is and which route the animals use to reach the living space.

Inside the house we look in the kitchen, the utility room and any room where you heard or saw activity. Gaps around pipes under sinks, behind appliances and around the water heater are common routes between floors.

Seasons and rodent activity.

Summer is the quiet period indoors and the busy one outdoors. Rats and mice feed on fallen fruit, garden produce, pet food and compost, and burrows appear along fences and foundations. Yard habits in summer decide how many animals look for a way in later.
