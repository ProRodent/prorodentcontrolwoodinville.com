Most people find out about a dead rodent in the house with their nose. A smell arrives, strong and unpleasant, in one room or one corner, and it does not go away. It is a sign that an animal has died somewhere you cannot see.

This guide explains what the smell is, how long it typically lasts, how to find the source and what you can do. It also covers why it happens and how to stop it from happening again.

What causes the smell.

When an animal dies, its body begins to break down, and the process releases gases with a strong, sweet and rotten odor. In a sealed wall, an attic corner or a crawl space, those gases have nowhere to go but into the living space, often through gaps around outlets, vents and ducts.

Warm weather speeds the process, and cold slows it. Larger animals smell more than smaller ones, and a rat produces more odor than a mouse. Flies are a common companion, because they lay eggs on the carcass.

A rough timeline.

The smell usually starts within a few days of the death. It tends to peak in the first week or two and then fades as the body dries out. A mouse may smell for one to two weeks, and a rat for several. In cold, damp conditions the time can stretch.

Stage.What you notice.
First days.A faint sharp smell, sometimes with a few flies.
Peak, about a week.Strong, sweet and rotten, worst in warm rooms.
Fading.Smell lessens as the body dries.
Afterward.A lingering musty smell if stains or nesting remain.

These are rough figures, and conditions vary widely. If the smell lasts longer than a few weeks, there may be more than one animal, or other materials such as soiled insulation may be adding to it.

Finding the source.

Start with the strongest smell and with where flies gather. Check likely hiding places. In the attic, look in corners, along eaves and beneath insulation. In the crawl space, check near vents, pipes and along the foundation. Inside, look behind appliances, beneath cabinets and inside any panel you can open.

Walls are hardest. If the smell is strongest on a particular wall, tap and listen, and look for stains on the drywall. We use a bright light and an inspection camera to look through small openings, and we discuss any cutting before we do it.

  • Follow the flies, which gather near the source.
  • Check along the strongest smell and any ceiling or wall stain.
  • Look in the attic, crawl space and behind large appliances.
  • Check floor registers and ducts with a flashlight.
  • Do not tear into a wall before talking to someone.

Safe handling.

A dead rodent can carry bacteria, parasites and, in rare cases, disease. Wear gloves, avoid breathing dust and do not handle the animal with bare hands. Bag it in a sealed plastic bag, place it in another bag and put it in the trash, then wash your hands. Disinfect the area using a household disinfectant as public guidance describes.

If the animal is out of reach or the area is heavily soiled, call for help. See our page on dead rodent removal and odor control for how we do it.

Getting rid of the odor.

Once the animal is gone, the smell fades faster. Open windows, run fans and use an odor-neutralizing product on affected surfaces. Fragrance sprays mask the smell without removing it. Soiled insulation or carpet may need replacing, since it holds the odor.

Activated charcoal, baking soda and ventilation help with lingering odor in a room. A deep or persistent smell may need a professional treatment of the space.

A few honest limits.

We cannot always find the animal. Some are in sealed cavities, and cutting into every wall is rarely justified. In those cases, the practical choice is to wait out the smell while ventilating and neutralizing, and to make sure the entry points are closed so it does not recur.

If the smell is accompanied by stains, bubbling paint or a growing number of flies, the source is probably close and worth opening. If it is faint and fading, patience is a fair option. We explain the trade-offs at the inspection.

When it is an emergency.

A smell alone is not an emergency, but a rodent in living space, droppings in a kitchen or signs of chewed wiring are urgent. Call at any hour. The line is answered 24/7, and same-day visits are offered where the schedule allows.

Why it happened, and how to prevent a repeat.

A rodent dies in a wall for one of two reasons. It was trapped, or it ate loose poison and retreated to die. Both point to the same conclusion, which is that animals were living in the house. The right response is an inspection to find the entry points, followed by trapping and sealing.

Loose poison is a common cause of this problem, which is one reason we use trapping instead. See rodent trapping and removal. If you have used bait at home, tell us when you call.

What to do, and not do, while you wait

This is general public-health guidance from the CDC, not a description of any one job. It applies whether you call us or anyone else.

  • Skip the broom and the vacuum on dry droppings. Stirring them up is how dust gets airborne.
  • Air out a closed attic, garage or crawl space for at least 30 minutes before you go in to work.
  • Wear rubber or plastic gloves, and wash your hands well afterward, even if you wore gloves.
  • Soak the droppings and the surface in disinfectant first. Wiping comes only after the soak time.
  • Double check that waste is bagged and sealed, and never pick up a dead rodent with bare hands.
  • If you develop fever, muscle aches or trouble breathing in the weeks after cleaning up rodent droppings, tell a doctor about the exposure. We are not medical professionals and cannot judge individual health risk.

Sources and standards

These public sources sit behind the advice on this page. They cover standard practice and rules for everyone, ours included.

Common questions

Will the smell go away on its own?

Usually, within one to several weeks, but removing the source ends it much sooner.

Do I have to cut into the wall?

Not always. Many animals can be reached from an attic, crawl space or access panel.

Can I mask the smell?

Fragrance sprays only cover it. Finding and removing the source is the real fix.
