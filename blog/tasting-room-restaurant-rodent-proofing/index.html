Woodinville draws visitors to wineries, a distillery, a well-known garden and home store and dozens of tasting rooms and restaurants. Businesses that serve them face a set of rodent risks that homes do not. Food, waste, deliveries, shared walls and stored goods all draw animals.

This guide describes how rodents reach commercial spaces, what to look for and what a practical prevention routine includes. It is written for owners and managers, and it assumes you want a plan that fits busy trading hours.

Where the risks come from.

Commercial buildings have more openings than houses. Delivery doors open and close all day, dumpsters and bins sit outside, and utility lines pass through the walls at many points. Food and drink, even in a tasting room, leave crumbs, spills and packaging.

Shared buildings add another layer. Units in an office park or a strip of shops share walls, roofs and slabs, and a rodent can travel through hidden voids from one tenant to the next. A good habit in your own unit is not enough if the neighbor next door has a problem.

  • Delivery doors, roll-up doors and dock doors with worn seals.
  • Dumpsters and bins with gaps, open lids or messy pads.
  • Shared walls, ceiling voids and utility chases.
  • Floor drains and mop sinks that hold water and scraps.
  • Stored cardboard, bulk dry goods and packaging.
  • Landscaping and planters next to the walls.

Doors are the main route.

A door that is open for a delivery is open to anything outside. A closed door with a worn seal or a gap at the base is nearly as bad. Roll-up and garage-style doors have tracks and corners that rarely seal fully, which makes them a common entry in industrial-style units.

Check every exterior door at the base and sides. If you can see daylight beneath it, so can a mouse. New seals and brush strips are inexpensive, and doors with automatic closers reduce the time they stay open.

Waste and storage.

Waste is the biggest attractant. Keep dumpster lids closed and the pad clean. Take bins out at the end of service, not the morning after, and rinse them regularly. A dumpster pad with a gap at the base gives rats a shelter right next to the food.

Inside, store dry goods in sealed bins and keep boxes off the floor and away from walls. Rodents love cardboard and packaging, and a stack of cases is an ideal nest. A routine of first in, first out helps you spot damaged goods early.

Monitoring and records.

Prevention is easier to run if you know what is happening. A monitoring plan places stations or traps at key points, such as door lines, corners and behind equipment, and checks them on a schedule. Each visit is logged with what was found, what was done and what remains.

That record matters. If an inspector, a landlord or an insurer asks, you can show it. Our commercial rodent control page describes how monitoring and documentation work.

Handling a sighting.

If a customer or staff member sees a rodent.

  • Stay calm and note the time and location.
  • Do not use loose poison or glue boards in a food area.
  • Isolate and protect any exposed food.
  • Call the line and describe what happened.
  • Write the event in your pest log.

A quick, calm response protects both customers and your reputation. The line is answered 24/7, and same-day visits are offered where the schedule allows.

Working around trading hours.

Pest work in a business should not disrupt service. We plan visits for off hours where possible, use enclosed stations and traps rather than loose products in food areas, and explain what is placed and where. Tell us your hours, your busy periods and the layout when you call.

For the building envelope, see rodent exclusion. It covers sealing the gaps in walls, doors and roofs, which is the foundation of a lasting solution.

Staff habits that make a difference.

Even a well-sealed building fails if the staff leave a door propped open. A short briefing about closing doors, covering waste and reporting sightings makes a bigger difference than a new product. Make it a routine, and put the line number where everyone can find it.

A closing-time checklist.

  • Cover and remove food waste from the floor and counters.
  • Empty floor drain traps of debris and keep them covered.
  • Close and check delivery and roll-up doors.
  • Look along door lines for droppings or gaps.
  • Put cardboard in a closed bin, not on the floor.

Ten minutes at close of business protects the next day's trading and gives you something to record in the log.

Talk to your neighbors.

If you share a wall or a roof with another tenant, a conversation with them and with the landlord helps. A unit can be carefully sealed and still see activity from next door. Share what you have found and ask about their experience. A shared plan works better than separate ones.

What to do, and not do, while you wait

This is general public-health guidance from the CDC, not a description of any one job. It applies whether you call us or anyone else.

  • Skip the broom and the vacuum on dry droppings. Stirring them up is how dust gets airborne.
  • Open doors or vents and let an enclosed space air out for 30 minutes or more before cleanup.
  • Wear rubber or plastic gloves, and wash your hands well afterward, even if you wore gloves.
  • Soak the droppings and the surface in disinfectant first. Wiping comes only after the soak time.
  • Bag the waste, seal the bag, and put it in the trash. Do not handle live or dead rodents with bare hands.
  • Any fever, aches or breathing trouble after handling rodent waste should be checked by a doctor. We do not make health claims about individual exposure.
