It is two in the morning, the house is quiet, and something is moving above the ceiling. Few things are more unsettling. The good news is that the sound itself carries clues, and a few careful observations will tell you a lot about what is up there.

This guide helps you read attic noises, rule out harmless causes and decide when to call. We cannot diagnose from a distance, but better observations lead to a faster, more accurate visit.

Describe the sound before you guess.

Start with the type of sound. Light, quick scratching in short bursts often points to mice. Heavier thumping, rolling or scampering usually points to rats. A dragging or scraping sound can be a rat moving nesting material. Gnawing sounds are dull and repeated, as if someone were slowly scraping a board.

Then note the location. Sound that travels along a wall suggests an animal using a wall void. Sound across the middle of the ceiling suggests the attic floor. Sound near an eave or corner suggests an entry point nearby.

Finally, note the time. Rodents are mostly active after dark, with peaks in the first couple of hours after dusk and again before dawn. Daylight sounds often point to squirrels, birds or a mechanical cause.

Other causes worth ruling out.

  • Ductwork and framing that expand and contract with temperature changes, which creates ticking and popping.
  • Water pipes that knock or hum when a tap or appliance is used.
  • Wind moving loose flashing, vent covers or branches against the roof.
  • Birds nesting in a vent or soffit, usually active in daytime.
  • Squirrels, which are active by day and make heavier sounds.

A noise that stops when the heating or air conditioning cycles off is probably the building. A noise that follows a regular, nightly pattern and is accompanied by droppings is probably an animal.

Why attics are popular.

Attics are warm, dry, dark and quiet, with soft insulation to nest in. Heat from the living space rises into them. Most are rarely visited, so an animal can settle in for weeks without being disturbed. Many local attics have vents and gaps at the roof edge, and trees that reach the roof give animals an easy route.

Roof rats and squirrels reach attics from the outside, climbing limbs, wires and walls. Mice usually come up from below, through wall voids and gaps around pipes. That is one reason the entry points differ from one animal to the next.

What you can safely check yourself.

You do not need to go into the attic. In fact, we recommend that you do not, because disturbed insulation and droppings put particles in the air. From the ground, look at the roofline for loose or damaged boards, torn vent screens and gaps near the eaves. Look for branches near the roof and for droppings or stains beneath any suspected entry.

From inside, notice whether any smell has developed in the room below the sound. A sharp ammonia-like odor suggests urine. A heavier sweet-rotten smell suggests a dead animal. Mention both on the call.

Useful notes to take.

  • What the sound is like and how loud it is.
  • What time it starts and how long it lasts.
  • Where in the house it seems to come from.
  • Any smell, stain or droppings.
  • Whether it follows rain or a cold night.

How we separate rodents from other animals.

Several animals can sound alike. Squirrels are loud in daylight, bats make a faint chittering at dusk and raccoons produce heavy thumps and dragging. Rodents are lighter, mostly active after dark and leave droppings in small, regular shapes.

We look at the droppings, the entry points and the damage. A hole at the roof edge the size of a fist suggests something larger than a rat. A gap the width of a pencil along a pipe suggests a mouse. If the animal is not a rodent, we say so and tell you the right kind of professional to call.

Protecting insulation and wiring while you wait.

Insulation compressed into tunnels loses value, and urine soaks into it and holds odor. Wiring is a bigger worry. Rodents chew insulation off wires, and exposed wires can arc. If lights flicker, a breaker trips repeatedly or you smell something hot, treat that as an electrical emergency and call an electrician.

In the meantime, avoid storing items in the attic that rodents can nest in, such as boxes of clothes or paper, and keep the hatch closed.

When to call right away.

Call promptly if you hear sounds every night, find droppings, smell something sharp or sweet, or notice chewed wiring or insulation. Rodents chew wires, and chewed wiring is a fire risk. If you see sparks, smell burning or have flickering lights, call an electrician as well.

What to do, and not do, while you wait

This is general public-health guidance from the CDC, not a description of any one job. It applies whether you call us or anyone else.

  • Skip the broom and the vacuum on dry droppings. Stirring them up is how dust gets airborne.
  • Ventilate a shut-up space first. Half an hour of airing out is the CDC's starting point.
  • Gloves on first, handwashing last. Do both every time.
  • Wet droppings with a disinfectant or a bleach solution and let it soak for about five minutes before wiping with paper towels.
  • Bag the waste, seal the bag, and put it in the trash. Do not handle live or dead rodents with bare hands.
  • If you develop fever, muscle aches or trouble breathing in the weeks after cleaning up rodent droppings, tell a doctor about the exposure. We are not medical professionals and cannot judge individual health risk.

Sources and standards

These public sources sit behind the advice on this page. They cover standard practice and rules for everyone, ours included.

Common questions

Is it safe to go into the attic myself?

We advise against it when droppings are present. Disturbed insulation and droppings put particles in the air.

How do I tell squirrels from rats?

Squirrels are active in daylight and rats after dark. Describe the timing on the call.

Can sounds stop and still be a problem?

Yes. Animals may leave and return, or move to another part of the house. A visit confirms.
