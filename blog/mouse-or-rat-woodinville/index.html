Almost every call we take starts the same way. A homeowner describes droppings, a sound or a chewed object and asks whether it is a mouse or a rat. It is a fair question, because the treatment really does differ. In the Woodinville area there are several candidates, including house mice, deer mice and two kinds of rat, and they behave differently.

A phone description rarely settles it, and an in-person inspection does. Still, you can narrow it down before a technician arrives, and the details you share make the visit faster. This guide walks through the signs that separate the animals and explains why each answer changes the plan.

Droppings are the clearest clue.

Droppings are the first thing most people can look at from a safe distance. House mouse droppings are small, about the size of a grain of rice, pointed at the ends and scattered widely. You may find dozens in a single drawer or along a baseboard.

Rat droppings are larger. Norway rat droppings run around three quarters of an inch, with blunt, capsule-shaped ends, and they collect near burrows, along runs and in garages and crawl spaces. Roof rat droppings are a bit smaller, closer to half an inch, with pointed ends, and they turn up in attics, on top of walls and beneath trees where the animals feed.

Fresh droppings are dark and slightly shiny. Older ones dry out, turn gray and crumble. If you see fresh droppings, activity is current. Do not sweep or vacuum dry droppings, since that stirs up particles. Photograph them next to a coin for scale and describe them on the call.

What you hear and when.

Sound helps with location. Light, quick scratching in a wall or ceiling, often in short bursts, usually points to mice. Heavier thumping or rolling overhead at dusk or just after dark often points to rats in the attic. Many homeowners compare it to a small animal running across the ceiling.

Noises low in the house, under floors, along the slab or from a shed point toward Norway rats, which prefer ground level and tend to dig. A dull, repeated gnawing sound inside a wall can come from any of them.

Timing matters too. All of these animals are mostly active after dark. If you hear movement in broad daylight, a different animal such as a squirrel may be involved, and we will say so on the call.

Gnaw marks, nests and runs.

Gnaw marks tell you about size. Mice leave small, fine marks, often on food packaging and soft materials. Rats leave larger, rougher marks on wood, plastic and wiring insulation. Fresh gnawing is pale and clean, while older gnawing darkens.

Nests differ as well. Mice build small, loose balls of shredded paper, insulation and fabric, often tucked into drawers, boxes and wall voids. Rats build larger, messier nests, frequently in attics, crawl spaces and quiet corners of garages.

Runs and rub marks are the third clue. Rodents travel the same route every night, and the oils in their fur leave dark, greasy smears along walls, pipes and beams. Rats leave heavier marks than mice. A rub mark at the base of a wall tells you the route and often the entry.

  • Rice-sized droppings and light scratching suggest mice.
  • Larger droppings and heavy thumping suggest rats.
  • Holes about two inches wide beside a foundation suggest Norway rats.
  • Gnawed fruit or nuts and activity overhead suggest roof rats.
  • Greasy rub marks show a route in use.

Why the answer changes the plan.

Mice and rats look similar to a worried homeowner, but the tools and the schedule differ. Mice are curious and enter traps quickly, so a mouse job can finish within days. Rats are cautious and avoid new objects, so trapping takes patience and the first few days may show nothing at all.

The entry points differ too. A mouse needs a gap about a quarter inch wide, while a rat needs about half an inch. A rat can chew through soft materials a mouse cannot, which changes the materials we use to seal. See our mouse control and rat control pages for the full plans.

Deer mice and the wooded edge.

In and around Woodinville, deer mice are worth knowing about. They are common in wooded and rural settings, nest in sheds, woodpiles and garages, and often live closer to the forest edge than house mice. Public health guidance treats deer mouse droppings with particular care, so the advice not to sweep or vacuum applies even more strongly if you find droppings in a shed or cabin.

If your property backs onto a greenbelt, mention it on the call. It tells us the animal could be a deer mouse rather than a house mouse, and it changes where we look first.

How fast each one breeds.

Speed is one reason early action matters. Mice mature quickly and a female can have several litters in a year, each with a handful of young. Rats also produce several litters a year. One pregnant female in November can mean a family by February.

That is why we do not suggest waiting to see whether noises stop. A quiet week often means the animals have settled in and are building a nest, not that they have left.

What not to do while you wait.

  • Do not scatter loose poison, which can send animals to die inside walls.
  • Do not seal a gap you found before a technician has looked, since animals may be inside.
  • Do not sweep or vacuum dry droppings.
  • Do not leave pet food out overnight.
  • Do not assume a quiet night means the problem is gone.

Instead, store food, keep the area clear and write down what you notice. Those few habits give the inspection a head start and keep the situation from getting worse.

It also helps to keep a simple log for a few nights. Note the time, the sound and the part of the house, since a pattern is far more useful to a technician than a single remembered noise.

What to tell us on the call.

Four details help most. Where you saw or heard the activity, what the droppings look like, what time of night it happens and what borders your lot. A few photographs on your phone speed things up more than any description.

If you are still unsure after reading this, that is normal. Ask on the call, and the technician will confirm the animal on site before suggesting any plan. The first goal is always to identify the animal and the route it uses.

Before you call: safe steps for rodent signs

This is general public-health guidance from the CDC, not a description of any one job. It applies whether you call us or anyone else.

  • Do not sweep or vacuum dry droppings or nests. That can put contaminated dust in the air you breathe.
  • Open doors or vents and let an enclosed space air out for 30 minutes or more before cleanup.
  • Wear rubber or plastic gloves, and wash your hands well afterward, even if you wore gloves.
  • Soak the droppings and the surface in disinfectant first. Wiping comes only after the soak time.
  • Bag the waste, seal the bag, and put it in the trash. Do not handle live or dead rodents with bare hands.
  • Flu-like symptoms or shortness of breath after rodent exposure are a reason to see a doctor and mention the cleanup. Health questions belong with a physician, not a pest company.

Common questions

Can I tell mouse from rat by droppings alone?

Often, yes. Mouse droppings are about the size of a grain of rice, while rat droppings are noticeably larger. Photos next to a coin help.

Are deer mice common around Woodinville?

They live in wooded and rural settings across the Northwest, so properties near forest edges and greenbelts may see them.

Why does the animal matter?

Mice and rats need different trap placement, different timing and different sealing materials.
