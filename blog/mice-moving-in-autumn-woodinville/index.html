Every autumn, the calls we take follow a pattern. The first cool, damp weeks arrive, and soon after, people start hearing scratching in walls and finding droppings in garages. It feels sudden, but the animals have been nearby all along. The weather simply changes what they want.

This guide explains why autumn brings rodents indoors, where they usually enter a Woodinville house, and what you can do in late summer and early fall to make the move harder.

What the weather changes for a mouse.

Mice and rats need three things, which are food, shelter and warmth. In summer, outdoor food is plentiful and nights are mild, so there is little reason to go indoors. As autumn arrives, seeds and insects thin out, nights get cold and rain soaks the ground and vegetation.

A dry, warm space with food nearby becomes much more attractive than a wet nest in a hedge. Attics, garages, crawl spaces and wall voids all fit the description. Animals that have been living in your yard begin to explore the building, and they find any gap that is open.

Our region is wet for much of the year, so the rain-driven shift arrives early and lasts a long time. A mouse born in a woodpile in summer may be looking for winter shelter by October.

Where they get in around local homes.

Houses here are often wood-frame, with siding, trim and crawl spaces or daylight basements. Moisture softens trim and swells siding, and over the years small gaps open at corners and joints. Roofs collect moss and debris that hide gaps at the edge.

  • Crawl space vents with torn screens or missing covers.
  • Gaps around pipes and wires where they enter the wall.
  • Garage door seals that have flattened with age.
  • Soffits and fascia softened by moisture.
  • Gaps at the roof edge, chimney and attic vents.

The most common single entry we find is the garage. A flat door seal and a gap at the corner give a mouse all it needs. After that, they follow the wall into the house through the shared wall or a gap around a pipe.

The late-summer window.

The best time to act is before the first sustained rain. In late summer, the ground is dry, ladders are safe, and you can see gaps clearly. A quiet half-hour walking around the outside of the house pays off more than any trap in November.

Look at every place something passes through the wall. Check the screens at the crawl space vents, the seals on the garage door and the gap under side doors. Look up at the roof edge for loose boards or missing screens. If you have trees, look for limbs within a few feet of the roof.

Food and clutter as invitations.

Even a house with few gaps can be an easy target if there is food inside. Pet food left out overnight, open bags of seed and cereal in a pantry or garage give mice a reason to push through any gap they find. Stored cardboard and fabric give them nest material.

Fall is a good time to move food into sealed containers and to clear clutter along garage walls. Boxes pulled a few inches away from the wall let you see droppings early, and they give a technician room to work if you need a visit.

A late-summer checklist.

  • Walk the outside and note any gap larger than a pencil width.
  • Check crawl space vent screens and garage door seals.
  • Trim branches that touch the roof.
  • Move pet food and seed into sealed containers.
  • Pull stored boxes a few inches off garage walls.

Signs the move has already happened.

If you notice scratching at night, new droppings in the garage or pantry, chewed packaging or a faint musty smell, the move is already under way. Do not wait for it to stop on its own. A mouse that finds food and shelter will stay and breed, and a small problem becomes a large one in a few weeks.

Why a quiet house can still be a target.

A house does not need a visible problem to attract rodents. Warmth leaking from vents, the smell of cooking and the scent of stored food all drift outdoors, and a mouse that finds that signal follows it to the source. Even a very clean house can attract an animal if there is a gap to reach it.

The reverse is also true. A messy garage is not a problem if nothing can get inside. That is why the gaps matter more than anything else, and why a late-summer walk around the outside is worth more than any amount of indoor cleaning.

What to do in the first week of wet weather.

When the rain finally settles in, check the garage and the kitchen first. Look for fresh droppings, torn packaging and any new smell. Put a small piece of paper or a few dry crumbs near a suspect wall and see whether they disturb overnight. It is a crude test, but it tells you whether something is moving.

If you find signs, do not wait for them to fade. Early in the season the animals are new to the house and the number is small. A short, focused visit now often saves a longer one in midwinter.

After the first cold snap.

If you find activity, the order is trap, then seal. Sealing too early can lock animals inside, and trapping alone without sealing lets new animals replace the old ones. Plan on a follow-up visit to confirm the result. If the home is quiet for a couple of weeks, that is a good sign, and a spring check will confirm it.

Before you call: safe steps for rodent signs

This is general public-health guidance from the CDC, not a description of any one job. It applies whether you call us or anyone else.

  • Never dry sweep or vacuum droppings, urine stains or nesting material.
  • Air out a closed attic, garage or crawl space for at least 30 minutes before you go in to work.
  • Put on gloves before touching anything contaminated, then wash your hands thoroughly when you finish.
  • Spray the area with disinfectant, wait roughly five minutes, then wipe it up with paper towels instead of dry material.
  • Bag the waste, seal the bag, and put it in the trash. Do not handle live or dead rodents with bare hands.
  • Any fever, aches or breathing trouble after handling rodent waste should be checked by a doctor. We do not make health claims about individual exposure.

Common questions

When should I check my house for gaps?

Late summer, before the first sustained rain, is ideal. The ground is dry and gaps are easy to see.

Why do mice enter garages first?

Garages are cluttered, rarely checked and often have worn door seals, which makes them an easy way in.

Is it too late once I hear noises?

No. Call and describe what you hear. Early action keeps a small problem small.
